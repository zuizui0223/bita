# Ecology Letters Synthesis proposal v2

**Provisional title:** Access and exposure domains organize floral defence selectivity and interaction routing

Flowers must remain accessible to mutualists while limiting antagonists that use the same structures and rewards. We propose a Synthesis asking a general ecological question: **when can a trait restrict antagonists without imposing the same cost on mutualists, and when does restriction instead reroute exploitation?** We develop an effective-exposure framework in which antagonists and mutualists differ in access, susceptibility, timing and response thresholds. The model predicts a selective window when antagonist effects occur before mutualist interference, collapse of selectivity under strong domain overlap, failure under bypass, and route switching when exploiters circumvent an access barrier.

We test these predictions with four linked evidence layers. First, a deduplicated D-side route corpus contains 17 unique study programs spanning chemical (9), physical (7) and reward/access (1) implementations; among the 10 programs that also measured pollinator consequences, responses are predominantly context-dependent or low-cost rather than one fixed penalty. Second, a stricter matched-system analysis yields 11 scorable systems under a fixed ecological-state mapping: all nine historical and both post-rule systematic-expansion systems fall into the predicted separated, transitional or overlapped state families, whereas a coarse defence-modality classifier recovers only 6/9 historical systems by leave-one-out prediction and 1/2 expansion systems. Third, eight independent systems show within-trait switching with dose, cumulative exposure, consumer identity, response stage or temporal expression. Fourth, two independent public network analyses recover the access-routing prediction in different faunas. In an Afrotropical insect–flower network, tube length is associated with a shift from thieving through the floral opening toward nectar robbing by bypass among 57 cheating-exposed plant species (Spearman rho = 0.347; permutation p = 0.0086); a source-defined multitrait sensitivity limits the inference to access geometry rather than tube length alone. In an independent all-Ecuador extension of the Aubert et al. bird–flower trait-barrier framework, 1,378 bird × plant × site units show mean robbery of 0.307 when flower tubes exceed bird bills versus 0.081 when accessible; 15 of 17 comparable sites show the same direction (sign-test p = 0.00235; site-stratified permutation p = 0.0001).

The synthesis links threshold theory, source-audited comparative evidence and a quantitative community-scale test. Its broader principle is that mutualist–antagonist outcomes can be understood as **interaction routing through unequal access and exposure domains**, rather than as fixed costs of a trait class. We explicitly retain identification limits and do not claim a universal causal coefficient or prevalence estimate.

The lead author’s research focuses on plant–pollinator interactions, floral trait evolution and quantitative ecological synthesis, and the evidence base and public-data reanalysis are already assembled in a reproducible workflow.

**Provisional title:** Access and exposure domains organize floral defence selectivity and interaction routing

Flowers face a general routing problem: the same structures and rewards must remain accessible to mutualists while limiting antagonists. We propose a Synthesis asking when a trait can filter antagonists selectively, when it instead interferes with mutualists, and when barriers reroute rather than stop exploitation. We develop an effective-exposure framework in which consumers differ in access, susceptibility, timing and response thresholds. The framework predicts selective windows under domain separation, interference under overlap, failure under bypass, and route switching under access mismatch.

We triangulate these predictions across four evidence layers. A deduplicated floral-defence corpus contains 17 study programs spanning chemical, physical and reward/access implementations, with heterogeneous rather than uniformly costly pollinator consequences. A matched-system analysis recovers a fixed separated–transitional–overlapped state mapping in nine historical-derivation plus two systematic-expansion systems; because the strict direction-supported subset is only n=3, we treat this as state recovery rather than a confirmatory success rate. Eight independent systems show within-trait switching with dose, exposure, consumer identity, response stage or timing. Two public network analyses independently recover the routing prediction: in Afrotropical insect–flower networks, access geometry is associated with robbing versus thieving among 57 plant species (Spearman rho=0.347, permutation p=0.0086); in an all-Ecuador bird–flower extension, robbery is higher when flower tubes exceed bird bills across 1,378 bird × plant × site units (0.307 versus 0.081), with the same direction in 15 of 17 comparable sites.

The synthesis proposes a broader ecological principle: mutualist–antagonist outcomes emerge from interaction routing through unequal access and exposure domains, not fixed costs of trait classes. We retain causal and non-pooling boundaries across evidence types. The lead author works on plant–pollinator interactions, floral trait evolution and quantitative ecological synthesis; all analyses are assembled in a reproducible workflow.

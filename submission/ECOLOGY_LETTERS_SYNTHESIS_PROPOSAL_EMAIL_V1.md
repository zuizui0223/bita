**Provisional title:** Access and exposure domains organize floral defence selectivity and interaction routing

Flowers face a general design problem: traits must remain accessible to mutualists while limiting antagonists that exploit the same structures and rewards. We propose a Synthesis asking when a trait can restrict antagonists without imposing the same cost on mutualists, and when restriction instead reroutes exploitation. We develop an effective-exposure framework in which consumers differ in access, susceptibility, timing and response thresholds, predicting selective windows, interference under overlap, failure under bypass, and route switching under access mismatch.

We test these predictions with four linked evidence layers. First, a deduplicated D-side corpus contains 17 study programs spanning chemical, physical and reward/access implementations; pollinator consequences in 10 same-study follow-ups are heterogeneous rather than uniformly costly. Second, 11 scorable matched systems all fall into a fixed separated–transitional–overlapped state mapping, whereas a coarse defence-modality classifier performs less consistently. Third, eight independent systems show within-trait switching with dose, exposure, consumer identity, response stage or timing. Fourth, two independent public networks recover the same routing direction. In Afrotropical insect–flower networks, access geometry is associated with robbing versus thieving among 57 plant species (Spearman rho = 0.347, permutation p = 0.0086). In an Ecuadorian bird–flower extension, robbery is higher when flower tubes exceed bird bills across 1,378 bird × plant × site units (0.307 versus 0.081), with the same direction in 15 of 17 comparable sites.

The synthesis links threshold theory, source-audited comparative evidence and cross-fauna network tests to propose a broader principle: mutualist–antagonist outcomes can be understood as interaction routing through unequal access and exposure domains. The lead author works on plant–pollinator interactions, floral trait evolution and quantitative ecological synthesis; the evidence base and public-data analyses are already assembled in a reproducible workflow.

# Ecology Letters Letter cover letter v1

**Article type:** Letter

**Provisional title:** Access constraints predict interaction routing across bird and insect visitor networks

Dear Editors,

Please consider our manuscript, “Access constraints predict interaction routing across bird and insect visitor networks,” as a Letter in *Ecology Letters*.

The manuscript asks a narrower question than the source studies: how does relational access mismatch affect two distinct margins of an interaction network—**which route consumers use and whether feeding occurs at all**—and does raw morphological equality identify the functional access boundary?

Our primary quantitative test uses an all-Ecuador bird–flower network. Across 259 plant species, mean tube–bill mismatch was positively associated with robbery (rho=0.503, p=0.0001), and the direction persisted within bird species (28 species / 1,285 bird × plant dyads, rho=0.340, p=0.0001). A preregistered zero-inclusive analysis using the source paper's binary tube>culmen barrier initially gave higher pooled feeding (rate ratio 1.772, 95% CI 1.105–2.841), with a post-open split showing legitimate/non-robbing 1.883 (1.111–3.190) and robbery-only 0.463 (0.147–1.461). We then tested whether culmen equality is a functional access boundary. In a post-open Trochilidae-only sensitivity, the 80% tongue-extension correction previously used as a primary hummingbird-network convention reversed the participation result: pooled feeding 0.229 (0.147–0.355) and legitimate feeding 0.154 (0.087–0.271), while robbery-only counts remained unresolved at 0.816 (0.359–1.857). The continuous routing association was unchanged and the paired robbery contrast strengthened. Thus route composition is robust to reach scaling, whereas the filtering inference is not.

We then asked whether a smaller, independently assembled Afrotropical insect network recovered the same routing direction. Among 57 plant species, floral tube length was associated with a shift from nectar thieving toward robbing (rho=0.347, permutation p=0.0086). A source-defined multitrait sensitivity did not isolate tube length as a unique partial predictor, so we interpret this as corroboration of an access-geometry association rather than a unique tube-length effect.

The two datasets were never pooled at the observation level. Each contributes one plant-species rank effect with equal network weight, yielding rho_J=0.428 and permutation p=0.0001. Because the joint statistic contains only k=2 independent standardized networks, we do not estimate between-network heterogeneity or claim generality beyond these two systems. Separately, a third independent multispecies Hawaiian bird–lobelioid dataset published by Case et al. (2026) reports the same directional relationship between bill–flower mismatch and nectar robbery; because that direction was already public and its effect has not been harmonized to our network statistic, we treat it only as directional corroboration.

We do not claim that trait–robbery associations, tactic switching, or relative handling-cost logic are new. The contribution is the **network-scale separation of route composition from interaction occurrence**, the within-consumer relational test, and the demonstration that these margins behave differently when morphology is translated into function. Continuous mismatch robustly predicts routing, but the source-defined bill-equality barrier gives the opposite participation conclusion from a literature-grounded hummingbird reach correction. This identifies raw trait equality as an inadequate functional threshold rather than treating “barrier” as self-evident. Across the independent insect and bird systems, the standardized routing direction recurs; a frozen outcome-blind frame of 33 direct programs supplies positive, null, opposite and mixed boundary cases without being converted into a success-rate argument.

All analyses and aggregate outputs are reproducible from public data and repository workflows.
